# 5. The Disputed Prophecies

*Part 5 of 6 in a series on the main points of dispute between Ahmadi Muslims and mainstream Muslims. It describes the debate; it does not decide it.*

> **Note on verse numbers.** Standard numbering is used. In an Ahmadi Quran most verses are one number higher.

## 1. Two ways of judging a prophecy

### The critics' test

- A true prophet's predictions do not fail (Deuteronomy 18:22). The Quran says God reveals the unseen to messengers He chooses (72:26–27).
- Mirza Ghulam Ahmad himself presented these prophecies as decisive tests of his truth. Reading conditions into them after the deadlines passed is, critics say, special pleading.

### The Ahmadi framework

- **A promise (*waʿd*) is different from a warning (*waʿīd*).** God never breaks a promise of good, but He may hold back a threatened punishment if people repent. Many Ashʿarī theologians accepted this principle: forgoing a threat (*khulf al-waʿīd*) is not a defect in God.
- **Quranic examples:** the people of Jonah were spared the announced punishment when they believed (10:98); Pharaoh's people had punishment lifted several times when they asked Moses to pray for them (7:134–135; 43:49–50); and the believer at Pharaoh's court said that if Moses is truthful, "*some* of what he warns you of will befall you" (40:28).
- **Biblical examples:** Nineveh was spared after Jonah's "forty days" (Jonah 3); King Hezekiah was told he would die, then given fifteen more years (Isaiah 38:1–5); and Jeremiah 18:7–10 states the rule that God relents when a nation turns back.
- **Prophecies are often fulfilled in an unexpected way.** The Prophet's dream of entering Mecca came true a year later than his Companions expected, after the Treaty of Hudaybiyya (48:27). His wives took "the one with the longest arm will be the first to join me" literally, but it meant the most generous of them (al-Bukhārī 1420; Muslim 2452).

## 2. Abdullah Atham (1893–1896)

### Background

Abdullah Atham was a retired government official in Amritsar and a well-known Christian debater; he had been born a Muslim and become a Christian. Ahmadi writers note that he had called the Prophet a *dajjāl* (deceiver) in his book *Andruna-e-Bible*. From 22 May to 5 June 1893 he and Mirza Ghulam Ahmad held a written debate in Amritsar on the divinity of Christ, later published as *Jang-e-Muqaddas* ("The Holy War").

### The prophecy (5 June 1893)

At the end of the debate Mirza Ghulam Ahmad announced that whichever side was knowingly following falsehood and making a weak human being into God would, within fifteen months (one month for each day of the debate), be thrown into *hāwiya* and suffer severe disgrace, provided he did not turn towards the truth. He added that if the prophecy proved false he was ready to accept any punishment, even hanging.

### What happened

- The fifteen months ended on 5 September 1894. Atham was alive, and Christians paraded him through Amritsar the next day.
- Mirza Ghulam Ahmad replied (in *Anwar-ul-Islam*, September 1894) that Atham had met the condition. For fifteen months he had been visibly afraid, had stopped attacking Islam, had moved from city to city, and had claimed there were attempts on his life.
- In a series of seven announcements he challenged Atham to declare on oath, in public, that he had not turned towards the truth during that time. He offered a reward that rose with each announcement, and said that if Atham swore and did not die within a year, he himself would be proved false.
- Atham refused to take the oath, citing Christian objections to oaths.
- After the seventh announcement (30 December 1895), Atham died in Firozpur on 27 July 1896, about seven months later. Ahmadis see this as the second stage: respite while he was afraid, death once he hid it.

### The arguments

- **Critics:** the natural meaning of the prophecy was death within fifteen months. "Turning to the truth" cannot be checked, and Atham stayed a Christian to the end. His fear is explained by the tense atmosphere (he said he feared assassination). The offer to be hanged shows how plainly the prophecy was meant.
- **Ahmadis:** the condition was in the original wording, not added later. Atham's own behaviour showed it was met, and his refusal to swear speaks for itself; he did suffer the downfall foretold. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad contrasted his case with that of Lekh Ram (section 5): Atham softened and was given respite; Lekh Ram did not.

## 3. Ahmad Beg's family and Muhammadi Begum (1886–1890s)

### Background

Mirza Ahmad Beg of Hoshiarpur was a close relative by marriage of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's family. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad described members of this branch of the family as irreligious: they mocked Islam and the Prophet and demanded a sign. In the mid-1880s Ahmad Beg needed Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's consent for the transfer of land belonging to a missing relative. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad said that he prayed about it and was told by revelation to ask for the hand of Ahmad Beg's elder daughter, Muhammadi Begum.

### The prophecy

Announced between 1886 and 1888 (notably in a leaflet of July 1888):

- If Ahmad Beg agreed, it would bring blessings to his family.
- If he married her to someone else, he would die within three years, and her husband within two and a half years of the wedding, and misfortune would fall on the family.
- In the end she would be married to Mirza Ghulam Ahmad. (Revelations he reported in this period include "We have married her to you".)
- A revelation in the same series called on a woman of the family to "repent, repent" (*tūbī, tūbī*), because misfortune was hanging over her descendants. Ahmadis treat this as the condition attached to the whole prophecy.

### What happened

- Muhammadi Begum was married to Mirza Sultan Muhammad of Patti in 1892.
- Ahmad Beg died on 30 September 1892, a few months after the wedding and well within the three years.
- Sultan Muhammad did not die within two and a half years; he lived until the late 1940s. Muhammadi Begum died in the 1960s. She never married Mirza Ghulam Ahmad.

### The arguments

- **Critics:** the two central predictions, the husband's death and the marriage, failed. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad described the marriage as God's decree and, in writings of the 1890s, staked his truthfulness on the rest of the prophecy. Critics also point to pressure within his own family: in 1891 he broke with relatives who sided with Ahmad Beg's family, including his son from his first marriage and that son's mother.
- **Ahmadis:**
  - It was a *warning* prophecy with a clear condition. The marriage depended on the husband's death, and the husband's death depended on the family continuing in defiance.
  - After Ahmad Beg's death the family was terrified and turned to prayer. Many of its members, including Muhammadi Begum's mother, later took the bai'at, and Ahmadi sources report that one of her sons joined the community.
  - In *Haqiqat-ul-Wahi* (1907) Mirza Ghulam Ahmad explained that the prophecy had two branches, both conditional, and that the husband's fear had put off his death. He proposed a test: let Sultan Muhammad publicly deny that he had been afraid, and see whether he survived the period God would set.
  - In a 1921 interview published in the Ahmadi newspaper *al-Fazl*, Sultan Muhammad spoke respectfully of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad.
  - The Lahore branch stresses the prophecy's purpose, the reform of a family hostile to religion, which it sees as achieved.

## 4. Sanaullah Amritsari (1907–1908)

### Background

Maulana Sanaullah Amritsari (1868–1948) was an Ahl-e-Hadith scholar and debater. He edited the weekly *Ahl-e-Hadith* and, from 1907, a monthly focused on the Ahmadiyya controversy, *Muraqqaʿ-e-Qadiyan*. He was one of the movement's most determined opponents. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad had included him in a general challenge to a prayer-duel (*mubāhala*) in *Anjam-e-Atham* (1897).

### The exchange of 1907

- In his paper of 29 March 1907, Sanaullah wrote that he was ready for both sides to pray that whichever of them was in the wrong should die in the lifetime of the one who was right.
- The Ahmadi paper *Badr* (4 April 1907) announced that Mirza Ghulam Ahmad accepted.
- On 15 April 1907 Mirza Ghulam Ahmad published *"The final decision with Maulvi Sanaullah"*. He wrote that if he was the liar and impostor Sanaullah described, he would die in Sanaullah's lifetime. If he was from God, he hoped that Sanaullah would not escape, in Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's lifetime, the kind of punishment that comes from God rather than from human hands (a deadly disease such as plague or cholera), unless he repented. He said this was not a prophecy based on revelation but a prayer asking God to decide, and invited Sanaullah to print the notice in his paper with any reply he wished.
- Sanaullah's paper (19 and 26 April 1907) rejected the arrangement. He said he had offered to take an oath, not to enter a *mubāhala*, and argued that God gives liars and wrongdoers a long life so that they may add to their wrongs (citing 19:75), pointing out that the Prophet died before the false prophet Musaylima.
- Mirza Ghulam Ahmad died in Lahore on 26 May 1908, about thirteen months later. Sanaullah died in Sargodha on 15 March 1948.

### The arguments

- **Critics:** he framed it as a prayer to *God*, not as a contract needing Sanaullah's agreement, and the result matched the test he himself set. Critics also say he died of an illness with diarrhoea and vomiting; they cite an account attributed to his father-in-law, Mir Nasir Nawab, in which he is reported to have spoken of having cholera; and they quote reports in the Ahmadi press of the time which they read as showing that he believed the prayer had been accepted.
- **Ahmadis:**
  - A *mubāhala* needs both sides (3:61). Sanaullah raised the idea, then publicly withdrew, so the arrangement never came into force.
  - Mirza Ghulam Ahmad expressly said this was a prayer, not a revealed prophecy.
  - By Sanaullah's own published argument, a long life proves nothing, since liars are given respite.
  - On the cause of death: Ahmadi accounts say the Civil Surgeon of Lahore certified that he died of a non-infectious intestinal illness (dysentery or diarrhoea). That, they say, is why the railway allowed his body to be taken to Qadian by train, which would not have been permitted for a cholera death.

## 5. Prophecies Ahmadis regard as fulfilled

Ahmadis often set these against the disputed ones.

- **Pandit Lekh Ram**, a leader of the Arya Samaj who wrote harshly against Islam and the Prophet. In an announcement of 20 February 1893, Mirza Ghulam Ahmad foretold a terrible punishment for him within six years. Lekh Ram was murdered in Lahore on 6 March 1897, the day after Eid al-Fitr, which Ahmadis link to an accompanying revelation. The killer was never identified. Critics hinted at foreknowledge; the police searched Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's house in Qadian, and Ahmadis note that nothing was found.
- **John Alexander Dowie**, an American preacher who claimed to be Elijah and attacked Islam. In 1902–03 Mirza Ghulam Ahmad challenged him through American newspapers to a prayer-duel, and Dowie mocked the challenge. Dowie suffered a stroke in 1905, was removed by his own followers in 1906, and died on 9 March 1907. *Critics:* Dowie never accepted, and his decline had ordinary causes.
- **The Promised Son (*Muṣliḥ Mawʿūd*).** The announcement of 20 February 1886 foretold a son with many great qualities. The main Jama'at identifies him as Mirza Bashiruddin Mahmud Ahmad (born 12 January 1889), who led the community from 1914 until 1965 and announced in 1944 that he was this son. The Lahore branch disputes the identification.
- **The plague.** From 1898 plague ravaged the Punjab. In *Kashti-e-Nuh* (1902) he announced God's promise to protect "all who are in this house", which Ahmadis understand as his household and his community, and the sparing of Qadian from devastating plague. *Critics* note that some Ahmadis, including in Qadian, died of plague; *Ahmadis* reply that the promise was protection from devastation, not from every single case.
- **The eclipses of 1894**: see Part 2.

## 6. Where this leaves the debate

Neither side seriously disputes the main facts and dates. The disagreement is about method. Critics read the plain wording, and his own confident statements, as the test. Ahmadis read the same texts as conditional warnings governed by God's practice of mercy, supported by Quranic and Biblical precedent.
