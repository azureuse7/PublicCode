# Schools of Thought in Islam

*A practical guide to the main branches and schools, how their practices differ, and how to tell which one someone follows.*

*Prepared 4 November 2026.*

> **Please note:** This is a general overview, not a religious ruling. Practices vary between scholars, countries and families, and many Muslims don't identify strongly with any single school. Where there is disagreement about who belongs to a group, this guide describes the positions without taking sides.

## 1. The big picture

"School of thought" means several different things in Islam, and the categories overlap. It helps to think in **layers**:

| Layer | The question it answers | Main examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Branch** | Who rightfully led the Muslim community after the Prophet, and where religious authority lies today | Sunni, Shia, Ibadi |
| **Legal school** (*madhhab*) | How to work out rules for worship, family life, food, money and so on | Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali (Sunni); Ja'fari (Twelver Shia) |
| **Theological school** (*aqidah*) | How to understand God's attributes, fate and free will | Ash'ari, Maturidi, Athari |
| **Spiritual path** (*tariqa*) | How to pursue inner, mystical devotion | Sufi orders such as Naqshbandi, Qadiri, Chishti, Shadhili |
| **Modern movement** | Reform and revival trends of the last 250 years or so | Salafi, Deobandi, Barelvi, Ahl-e-Hadith |

These layers stack. One person could be **Sunni**, follow **Hanafi** law, hold **Maturidi** theology, belong to a **Naqshbandi** Sufi order and attend a **Deobandi** mosque, all at once.

```text
Islam
├── Sunni (about 87–90% of Muslims)
│   ├── Legal schools: Hanafi · Maliki · Shafi'i · Hanbali
│   ├── Theology: Ash'ari · Maturidi · Athari
│   └── Modern movements: Salafi · Deobandi · Barelvi · Ahl-e-Hadith
├── Shia (about 10–13% of Muslims)
│   ├── Twelver (Ithna 'Ashari): Ja'fari law; by far the largest Shia branch
│   ├── Ismaili: Nizari (led by the Aga Khan) · Musta'li (e.g. Dawoodi Bohras)
│   └── Zaydi: mainly northern Yemen
├── Ibadi: mainly Oman, plus pockets of North and East Africa
└── Sufism: a spiritual dimension found within both Sunni and Shia Islam
```

The population shares are Pew Research Center estimates. They are approximate, because few censuses ask Muslims whether they are Sunni or Shia.

In 2005, the **Amman Message**, endorsed by prominent scholars from around the world, recognised eight legal schools as valid: the four Sunni schools, the Shia Ja'fari and Zaydi schools, the Ibadi school and the Zahiri school.

All Muslims share the core of the faith: belief in one God, in Muhammad as the final prophet and in the Qur'an, together with the five daily prayers, the Ramadan fast, almsgiving (*zakat*) and the Hajj pilgrimage. The differences are mostly about authority, history, law and ritual detail.

## 2. Sunni and Shia: how the main split began

The divide began with a disagreement over who should lead the community after the Prophet Muhammad died in 632 CE.

- **Sunnis** (from *Ahl al-Sunna wa'l-Jama'a*, "the people of the Prophet's way and of the community") accept the first four caliphs (**Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali**) as rightful leaders chosen by the community.
- **Shia** (from *Shi'at Ali*, "the party of Ali") believe the Prophet designated his cousin and son-in-law **Ali** as his successor, and that leadership belongs to a line of **Imams** from the Prophet's family, descended from Ali and the Prophet's daughter **Fatima**.
- The killing of Ali's son **Husayn** and his companions at **Karbala** in 680 CE became a defining event for Shia identity. It is commemorated every year on **Ashura** (10 Muharram).

| | Sunni | Shia (Twelver, the largest Shia branch) |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership after the Prophet | Caliphs chosen by the community | Imams from the Prophet's family, each designated by the one before |
| View of the Prophet's Companions | All are highly respected | More critical of some, especially over the succession |
| Religious authority today | Scholars (*ulama*) and muftis; no formal hierarchy | A hierarchy of jurists; ordinary believers follow a senior jurist (*marja' al-taqlid*) |
| Main hadith collections | Bukhari, Muslim and the other "Six Books" | The "Four Books" (e.g. *al-Kafi*) |
| Extra religious due | None on income | *Khums*: one-fifth of annual surplus income |
| Temporary marriage (*mut'ah*) | Not allowed | Allowed |
| Ashura | Many fast | Mourning for Husayn |
| Where they are a majority | Most Muslim-majority countries | Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain |

## 3. Sunni schools of law (madhhabs)

Mainstream Sunnis see the four schools as equally valid ways of interpreting the same sources: the Qur'an and the Prophet's example (*Sunnah*). They agree on the fundamentals and differ on details. Many scholars allow following another school's view on a particular issue, and one mosque often includes followers of different schools.

| School | Founder (died) | Distinctive approach | Where it's dominant today |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hanafi** | Abu Hanifa (767), Kufa, Iraq | The oldest and most widely followed school; gives a strong role to reasoning, including analogy (*qiyas*) and "juristic preference" (*istihsan*) | Turkey, the Balkans, Central Asia, Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, parts of the Levant and Iraq, and other former Ottoman and Mughal lands |
| **Maliki** | Malik ibn Anas (795), Medina | Gives weight to the established practice of the people of Medina and to public interest (*maslaha*) | North and West Africa, Sudan, Upper Egypt and parts of the Gulf states |
| **Shafi'i** | Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (820), Egypt | Systematised Islamic legal theory; a structured balance of texts and analogy | Lower Egypt, East Africa (e.g. Somalia), Yemen, Indonesia, Malaysia and the rest of maritime Southeast Asia, Kurdish regions, Sri Lanka, south-west India, the North Caucasus and parts of the Levant |
| **Hanbali** | Ahmad ibn Hanbal (855), Baghdad | Sticks most closely to the texts of the Qur'an and hadith; the most cautious about analogy | Saudi Arabia and Qatar; a strong influence on modern Salafism |

Other early schools, such as the literalist **Zahiri** school, have mostly disappeared or survive only at the margins.

## 4. Sunni schools of theology

| School | Founder (died) | Key idea | Most common among |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ash'ari** | Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (936) | Uses rational argument (*kalam*) to defend traditional beliefs; verses describing God in human terms are either read figuratively or their meaning is left to God | Shafi'i and Maliki scholars |
| **Maturidi** | Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (944), Samarkand | Very close to Ash'ari, with a somewhat larger role for human reason and free choice | Hanafis, especially in Turkey and Central and South Asia (including Deobandis and Barelvis) |
| **Athari** ("traditionalist") | Associated with Ahmad ibn Hanbal | Accepts the texts' descriptions of God as they are, "without asking how", and rejects speculative theology | Hanbalis and today's Salafis |

Most ordinary believers never think about these theological labels. They matter mainly to scholars and in debates between movements.

## 5. Shia branches

The Shia branches split over which descendant of Ali was the rightful Imam.

| Branch | Line of Imams | Leadership today | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Twelver** (*Ithna 'Ashari*) | Twelve Imams. The twelfth, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to have gone into hiding (*occultation*) in the 9th century and will return | Senior jurists (ayatollahs, *maraji'*); each believer chooses one to follow | Majorities in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain; large communities in Pakistan, India, Lebanon, Afghanistan, Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, Kuwait and elsewhere |
| **Ismaili** | Split from the Twelvers in the 8th century over who succeeded the sixth Imam, Ja'far al-Sadiq | **Nizari** Ismailis follow a living Imam, the **Aga Khan**. **Musta'li/Tayyibi** Ismailis, such as the **Dawoodi Bohras**, follow a leader called the *Da'i al-Mutlaq* (addressed as *Syedna*) while their Imam is in seclusion | Nizaris: South and Central Asia, East Africa, Syria and a large Western diaspora. Bohras: India (especially Gujarat and Mumbai), Pakistan, East Africa, Yemen and the diaspora |
| **Zaydi** (sometimes called "Fivers") | Follow Zayd ibn Ali (died 740). Any learned, pious descendant of Ali and Fatima who rises to lead can be Imam | No hidden Imam; led by scholars | Mainly northern Yemen |

- **Twelvers** follow **Ja'fari** law, named after the sixth Imam. Most are **Usuli**: they follow a living jurist who reasons from the sources. A small **Akhbari** minority relies more directly on hadith reports. Because each believer follows a particular jurist, two Twelvers can follow slightly different rulings.
- **Nizari Ismailis** emphasise the inner (*batin*) meaning of faith and the guidance of the living Imam. They gather in **jamatkhanas**, where their regular congregational prayer is a distinctive *du'a*.
- **Dawoodi Bohras** keep closely to formal ritual law and are recognisable by community dress. Men often wear a white cap embroidered with gold and a long white tunic; women wear a colourful two-piece *rida*.
- **Zaydis** are the closest Shia group to Sunnis in legal practice. They don't believe in a hidden Imam, and they reject temporary marriage.

## 6. Ibadi Islam

- Developed in **Basra** in the late 7th century. Ibadis regard the scholar **Jabir ibn Zayd**, from Oman, as their key early figure. Outsiders often trace them to the early **Kharijite** movement, a label Ibadis reject.
- Found mainly in **Oman**, where estimates put Ibadis at roughly 40–50% of the population and the ruling family is Ibadi. There are smaller communities in **Zanzibar**, Algeria's **M'zab valley**, the island of **Djerba** in Tunisia, and Libya's **Nafusa mountains**.
- Distinctive features: leadership (the imamate) is open to any qualified, pious Muslim rather than tied to lineage; Ibadis pray with their arms at their sides; and theologically they hold, among other things, that the Qur'an was created and that God will not be seen in the afterlife.

## 7. Sufism

- Sufism (*tasawwuf*) is the mystical, spiritual dimension of Islam, focused on purifying the heart and drawing closer to God. **It is not a separate sect.** Most Sufis are Sunni and follow one of the four legal schools, and there are Shia Sufi orders too.
- Sufis are organised into **orders** (*tariqas*). Each is led by a spiritual guide (*shaykh* or *pir*) with a chain of teachers going back to the Prophet.
- Well-known orders include the **Qadiri**, **Naqshbandi**, **Chishti**, **Shadhili**, **Tijani**, **Suhrawardi** and **Mevlevi** (the "whirling dervishes").
- Typical practices include group remembrance of God (*dhikr*), silent in some orders and chanted in others; devotional poetry and music, such as *qawwali* in the Chishti tradition; celebrating the Prophet's birthday (*Mawlid*); and visiting the shrines of saints.
- Salafis and some other reformists criticise certain popular Sufi practices, especially shrine devotion and asking saints to intercede.

## 8. Modern Sunni movements

These are reform or revival movements rather than legal schools. In everyday life they often matter more than the madhhab, because they shape which mosque someone attends and which customs they follow.

| Movement | Origins | Law and creed | Hallmarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Salafi** | Draws on Ibn Taymiyya (14th century) and Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (18th-century Arabia) | Prefers following evidence directly to strict loyalty to one school (in practice often close to Hanbali); Athari creed | Strong emphasis on pure monotheism (*tawhid*); rejects shrine devotion and Mawlid celebrations as innovations. Outsiders sometimes call Salafis "Wahhabis", a label most reject. Ranges from apolitical "quietists" to politically active groups |
| **Deobandi** | Darul Uloom seminary, Deoband, India (1866) | Hanafi; Maturidi | Seminary-based scholarship; accepts Sufism but opposes many popular devotional customs. The **Tablighi Jamaat** preaching movement (1926) grew out of this circle |
| **Barelvi** (*Ahl-e-Sunnat wa Jama'at*) | Ahmad Raza Khan of Bareilly, India (1856–1921) | Hanafi; Maturidi | Strongly devotional and Sufi-oriented: celebrates Mawlid, honours saints' shrines, recites poems in praise of the Prophet (*na'at*) and uses invocations such as "Ya Rasool Allah" |
| **Ahl-e-Hadith** | 19th-century India | Doesn't follow any one school; close to Salafi | Distinctive prayer: hands on the chest, "Ameen" said aloud, hands raised before and after bowing |

Political organisations such as the **Muslim Brotherhood** (Egypt, 1928) and **Jamaat-e-Islami** (South Asia, 1941) are movements, not schools of thought. Their members follow various legal schools.

## 9. Other groups, including those whose status is disputed

Whether some of these groups count as part of Islam is strongly contested. They are described here neutrally.

- **Ahmadiyya**: Founded in 1889 in Qadian, Punjab, by Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, whom followers regard as the Promised Messiah. Ahmadis identify as Muslims, but most Sunni and Shia scholars consider their beliefs about prophethood to place them outside Islam, and Pakistan's constitution declared them non-Muslims in 1974. There are two groups: the larger Ahmadiyya Muslim Community, led by a caliph, and the smaller Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement.
- **Alevis** (mainly Turkey; not the same as the Alawites): Revere Ali and the Twelve Imams and draw on Sufi (Bektashi) traditions. They worship in a *cemevi* rather than a mosque, and many don't follow the standard daily prayers or the Ramadan fast. Whether Alevism is a form of Shia Islam, a distinct Islamic tradition or a separate faith is debated, including among Alevis.
- **Alawites** (mainly Syria and southern Turkey): An esoteric tradition that grew out of Shia Islam, with many beliefs kept within the community. Their status is debated.
- **Druze** (Lebanon, Syria, Israel and Jordan): Emerged from Ismaili circles in the 11th century. Most Muslims and many Druze now regard it as a separate religion.
- **Quranists**: Treat the Qur'an as the only religious authority and reject the hadith collections.
- **Nation of Islam** (USA, 1930): A Black American movement whose teachings differ significantly from mainstream Islam. After 1975, many members followed Warith Deen Mohammed into mainstream Sunni Islam.

## 10. How practices differ: comparison tables

The core of worship is the same for everyone. The differences below are real, but they are mostly about details, and the Sunni schools treat each other's positions as valid alternatives. The "Twelver Shia" columns show the most common Ja'fari position; individual jurists vary.

### 10.1 Prayer (salah)

| Practice | Hanafi | Maliki | Shafi'i | Hanbali | Twelver Shia |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hands while standing | Folded below the navel (men); on the chest (women) | Usually arms at the sides | Folded between the chest and navel | Folded, usually below the navel | Arms at the sides |
| Raising hands, apart from the opening *Allahu akbar* | No | No (main view) | Yes, before and after bowing | Yes, before and after bowing | Yes, with each *Allahu akbar* (recommended) |
| "Ameen" after al-Fatiha, in prayers recited aloud | Said quietly | Said quietly | Said aloud | Said aloud | Not said |
| "Bismillah" before al-Fatiha, in prayers recited aloud | Said quietly | Usually left out | Said aloud | Said quietly | Said aloud |
| *Qunut* (supplication while standing) | In Witr, before bowing | In Fajr, before bowing | In Fajr, after bowing (and in Witr late in Ramadan) | In Witr, after bowing | In the second unit of every prayer |
| Status of Witr | Necessary (*wajib*); three units together | Strongly recommended (*sunnah*) | Strongly recommended | Strongly recommended | Recommended; part of the night prayer |
| What you prostrate on | Any clean surface | Any clean surface | Any clean surface | Any clean surface | Natural earth or plant material, usually a small clay tablet (*turbah* or *mohr*) |
| Combining prayers (Dhuhr with Asr, Maghrib with Isha) | Only at Arafah and Muzdalifah during Hajj | When travelling, in rain or in illness | When travelling or in rain | When travelling, in rain, in illness or other hardship | Allowed at any time, so many pray the five prayers in three sessions |
| Call to prayer (*adhan*) | Adds "Prayer is better than sleep" at Fajr | Same as Hanafi | Same as Hanafi | Same as Hanafi | Adds "Hasten to the best of deeds" and commonly a testimony that Ali is God's friend (*wali*); no "better than sleep" line |

- **Zaydis, Bohras and Ibadis** also usually pray with their arms at their sides.
- **Salafis and Ahl-e-Hadith** usually place their hands on the chest, say "Ameen" aloud and raise their hands before and after bowing, whatever school their family follows.
- After finishing the prayer, many Twelver Shia raise their hands three times while saying *Allahu akbar*.

### 10.2 Ablution (wudu) and purity

| Practice | Hanafi | Maliki | Shafi'i | Hanbali | Twelver Shia |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Feet | Washed | Washed | Washed | Washed | Wiped with wet hands |
| Wiping over leather socks (*khuffs*) instead of washing the feet | Allowed | Allowed | Allowed | Allowed | Not allowed, except in necessity |
| Head | Wipe at least a quarter | Wipe the whole head | Wipe any part | Wipe the whole head | Wipe the front part with the moisture left on the hand |
| Does touching someone of the opposite sex (outside close family) break wudu? | No | Only if done with desire | Yes, any skin contact | Only if done with desire | No |
| Does bleeding break wudu? | Yes, if blood flows | No | No | Yes, if it's a large amount | No |
| Does eating camel meat break wudu? | No | No | No | Yes | No |
| Dogs | Saliva is impure; the body is not | The dog is considered pure | Impure; anything licked is washed seven times, once with earth | Impure (as Shafi'i) | Impure |

Twelver Shia also wash the arms from the elbow down to the fingertips, while Sunnis usually start at the fingertips.

### 10.3 Fasting and Ramadan

| Practice | Sunni (all four schools) | Twelver Shia |
|---|---|---|
| Breaking the fast | At sunset | A little later, once the redness has left the eastern sky (roughly 10–15 minutes after sunset) |
| *Tarawih* (Ramadan night prayers) | Prayed in congregation; traditionally 20 units (Salafis and Ahl-e-Hadith usually pray 8) | Not prayed in congregation; other night prayers and supplications are done instead |
| Ashura (10 Muharram) | A recommended voluntary fast, often together with the 9th or 11th | A day of mourning for Husayn; fasting is generally discouraged |

### 10.4 Marriage, divorce and family

| Practice | Hanafi | Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali | Twelver Shia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does a woman need a guardian (*wali*) to marry? | No. An adult woman can contract her own marriage, though her guardian can object to an unsuitable match | Yes, a guardian is required | Not for a previously married adult woman; many jurists require a father's consent for a first marriage |
| Witnesses | Required for a marriage | Required for a marriage (Malikis emphasise public announcement) | Not required for a marriage; two witnesses are required for a divorce |
| Temporary marriage (*mut'ah*) | Not allowed | Not allowed | Allowed |
| Three divorces pronounced at once ("triple talaq") | Classically counts as a final, triple divorce | Classically counts as a final, triple divorce | Does not count as three |

Ibn Taymiyya, many modern Salafi scholars and the family laws of a number of countries count an instant triple talaq as a single divorce. India's Supreme Court struck down instant triple talaq in 2017.

### 10.5 Food

| Practice | Hanafi | Maliki | Shafi'i | Hanbali | Twelver Shia |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seafood | Fish only (prawns are debated) | Virtually all seafood | Virtually all seafood | Virtually all seafood | Only fish with scales, plus prawns and shrimp |
| Meat slaughtered by Jews or Christians | Permitted, with conditions | Permitted, with conditions | Permitted, with conditions | Permitted, with conditions | Not permitted (most jurists) |

All schools forbid pork and alcohol.

### 10.6 Money and religious dues

| Practice | Hanafi | Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali | Twelver Shia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zakat on gold or silver jewellery that is regularly worn | Due | Not due | Not due |
| *Khums* (one-fifth of annual surplus income) | Not paid on income | Not paid on income | Paid, typically through a senior jurist or his representatives |

### 10.7 Festivals and observances

| Occasion | Who marks it, and how |
|---|---|
| Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha | Everyone. Dates can differ by a day even within one group, depending on how the new moon is sighted |
| Ashura (10 Muharram) | Shia: mourning gatherings (*majalis*), processions and wearing black. Sunnis: an optional fast |
| Arba'een (40 days after Ashura) | Shia: mourning, and one of the world's largest pilgrimages, to Karbala |
| Eid al-Ghadir (18 Dhu al-Hijjah) | Shia: celebrates the Prophet's designation of Ali |
| Mawlid (the Prophet's birthday) | Celebrated by many Sunnis, especially Sufi and Barelvi communities, and by Shia, who usually mark it a few days later. Salafis and many Deobandis regard celebrating it as an innovation |
| Visiting shrines | Common among Shia (shrines of the Imams and their families) and Sufi-leaning Sunnis; opposed by Salafis |
| Imamat Day | Nizari Ismailis: the anniversary of the current Aga Khan becoming Imam |

### 10.8 South Asian Sunni movements at a glance

| Practice | Deobandi | Barelvi | Ahl-e-Hadith |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal school | Hanafi | Hanafi | No fixed school |
| Prayer style | Hanafi (hands below the navel, quiet "Ameen", no extra raising of hands) | Hanafi (same) | Hands on the chest, "Ameen" aloud, hands raised before and after bowing |
| Mawlid | Generally not celebrated | Celebrated enthusiastically | Not celebrated |
| Saints' shrines | Visiting is allowed, but shrine festivals (*urs*) and seeking saints' help are opposed | An important part of devotional life | Opposed |
| Standing to send salutations on the Prophet (*salat-o-salam*) after prayers | No | Common | No |
| *Tarawih* | 20 units | 20 units | 8 units |

## 11. How to tell which school someone follows

### Start by asking, and don't assume

- **Outward signs are unreliable.** People mix practices, follow local custom, change over time, or simply pray the way their family taught them. Converts and many younger Muslims pick up practices from different teachers and sources.
- **Many Muslims don't use a label.** In Pew surveys, many Muslims in some countries (Indonesia, for example) described themselves as "just a Muslim" rather than Sunni or Shia.
- **Sunni legal schools are not rival denominations.** Followers of all four often pray in the same mosque, and few ordinary believers treat their madhhab as an identity.
- **It's personal information.** In some countries, minorities such as Shia, Ismailis and Ahmadis face discrimination or violence, so people may prefer not to say. Respect that.

Natural ways to ask:

- "Which mosque or centre do you usually go to?"
- "Do you follow a particular madhhab or scholar?"
- "Is there anything I should know about, for example food, prayer times or fasting?"

### Clues that can suggest, but never confirm, an affiliation

| Clue | What it may suggest |
|---|---|
| **Where they worship** | *Masjid* (mosque): any group. *Imambargah* or *husayniyya*: Twelver Shia. *Jamatkhana*: Ismaili. *Cemevi*: Alevi. *Khanqah*, *zawiya* or *tekke*: Sufi lodge. A seminary called *Darul Uloom*: often (not always) Deobandi |
| **Who they look to for guidance** | A *marja'* or ayatollah: Twelver Shia. The Aga Khan: Nizari Ismaili. The *Syedna*: Dawoodi Bohra. A *pir* or *shaykh* they have pledged to: a Sufi order. Scholars of Deoband or Bareilly: Deobandi or Barelvi |
| **Arms at their sides in prayer** | Twelver Shia, Maliki, Zaydi, Ibadi or Bohra |
| **Hands folded below the navel** | Usually Hanafi (men), or Hanbali |
| **Hands on the chest, "Ameen" aloud, hands raised before and after bowing** | Shafi'i, Hanbali, Salafi or Ahl-e-Hadith |
| **Prostrating on a small clay tablet** | Twelver Shia |
| **Praying the five prayers in three sessions** | Twelver Shia |
| **Breaking the fast 10–15 minutes after sunset** | Twelver Shia |
| **Mourning and wearing black in Muharram, especially on Ashura** | Shia |
| **Celebrating Mawlid with processions or *na'at* gatherings** | Barelvi or another Sufi-leaning Sunni group, or Shia |
| **Greetings such as "Ya Ali madad"** | Shia or Ismaili (sometimes Sufi) |
| **Family background** | Only a rough hint (see the "Where it's dominant" columns above), and every country has minorities |

**Names and surnames are a poor guide.** Most common Muslim names are used across all groups, so don't rely on them.

### Two examples

> A man who prays with his hands folded below his navel, says "Ameen" quietly, goes to a mosque attached to a *Darul Uloom* seminary and doesn't celebrate Mawlid is *probably* a Deobandi-leaning Hanafi Sunni.
>
> A woman praying with her arms at her sides could be Maliki (for instance, of Moroccan or West African heritage) or Twelver Shia. If she prostrates on a clay tablet, Shia is much more likely.
>
> In both cases, the only way to know for sure is if they tell you.

## 12. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Aqidah* | Creed; theology |
| Ashura | The 10th day of the month of Muharram |
| Ayatollah | A senior Twelver Shia jurist |
| Caliph (*khalifa*) | A successor to the Prophet as leader of the community |
| *Fiqh* | Islamic jurisprudence (law) |
| Hadith | A report of the Prophet's words or actions |
| Imam | A prayer leader; for Shia, also a divinely guided leader from the Prophet's family |
| *Khums* | A Shia religious due of one-fifth of annual surplus income |
| *Madhhab* | A school of law |
| *Marja' al-taqlid* | "Source of emulation": a senior Twelver jurist whom believers follow |
| *Mawlid* | The Prophet's birthday |
| *Mut'ah* | Fixed-term (temporary) marriage, accepted by Twelver Shia |
| *Qunut* | A supplication made while standing in prayer |
| *Rak'ah* | One unit of prayer |
| *Sunnah* | The Prophet's example; also a recommended act |
| *Taqlid* | Following a school's or scholar's rulings |
| *Tariqa* | A Sufi order |
| *Turbah* / *mohr* | A small clay tablet used by Shia in prostration |
| *Wudu* | Ritual washing before prayer |
