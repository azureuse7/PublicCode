# 6. Jihad, Colonial-Era Politics and Loyalty to British Rule

*Part 6 of 6 in a series on the main points of dispute between Ahmadi Muslims and mainstream Muslims. It covers what jihad means, the conditions on armed struggle, Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's position under British rule, and the objections to it. It describes the debate; it does not decide it.*

> **Note on verse numbers.** Standard numbering is used. In an Ahmadi Quran most verses are one number higher.

---

## At a glance

| Question | Mainstream Muslims and critics | Ahmadis (both branches) |
|---|---|---|
| What does jihad mean? | Striving in several forms; armed struggle is one form, under strict conditions | The same |
| Did he abolish jihad? | He declared armed jihad forbidden, in his capacity as Messiah, at a time when this suited the colonial power | He taught that the conditions for *armed* jihad did not exist and that his age called for jihad by argument; defensive fighting under lawful authority remains valid |
| Was Mirza Ghulam Ahmad pro-British? | Yes, excessively, in his own words; his family had served the British | He praised British rule for the religious freedom it brought after Sikh rule, as most Muslim leaders of his time did; loyalty to any government that allows freedom of faith is a religious duty |
| Was the movement a "British plant"? | His own phrase "self-planted sapling" says so | The phrase described his family's record of loyalty, not his mission; British authorities investigated and prosecuted him, and he attacked the rulers' religion all his life |

---

## A note before starting

This topic produces more misunderstanding than any other in the series, in both directions. Ahmadis are sometimes accused of "abolishing jihad", which is not their claim. Mainstream Muslims are sometimes accused of endorsing aggressive warfare, which is not their position either. The doctrinal disagreement is narrow and technical: whether the conditions permitting armed struggle were suspended for the era of the Messiah. The historical disagreement is about how to read Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's loyalty to British rule.

Both communities condemn terrorism, the killing of non-combatants and forced conversion. That is not in dispute and is worth saying early in any discussion.

# Part One: What jihad means

## 1. The word itself

*Jihad* comes from the root *j-h-d*, meaning to strive or exert effort. It is **not** the Arabic word for war. That word is *qitāl* (fighting) or *ḥarb* (war), and the Quran uses them distinctly.

Classical scholarship recognises several forms:

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Jihad al-nafs* | striving against one's own soul and lower desires |
| *Jihad al-shayṭān* | striving against the promptings of Satan |
| *Jihad bil-lisān / bil-qalam* | striving by the tongue and the pen: argument, teaching, writing |
| *Jihad bil-māl* | striving by wealth: spending in the cause of Allah |
| *Jihad bis-sayf (qitāl)* | armed struggle under defined conditions |

Both communities accept that all of these exist. The dispute concerns only the last.

## 2. The mainstream position

Armed jihad is a legitimate category of Islamic law, subject to strict conditions, and it has not been abrogated or suspended.

### The Quranic basis

> **2:190**: "And fight in the cause of Allah against those who fight against you, but do not transgress. Surely, Allah loves not the transgressors."
>
> **22:39–40**: "Permission to fight is given to those against whom war is made, because they have been wronged… those who have been driven out from their homes unjustly only because they said, 'Our Lord is Allah.'"
>
> **8:61**: "And if they incline towards peace, incline thou also towards it."
>
> **2:256**: "There is no compulsion in religion."
>
> **60:8**: "Allah forbids you not, respecting those who have not fought against you on account of religion, that you be kind to them and deal equitably with them."

The classical reading of this material is that fighting is permitted as a response to aggression and to the violent suppression of religion, and is bounded on all sides.

### The legal conditions

The *fiqh* of *siyar* (international conduct) developed detailed constraints:

- It is declared only by legitimate authority, not by individuals or private groups.
- It needs a just cause: principally defence against attack, or against persecution that prevents worship.
- Non-combatants are immune: the Prophet forbade the killing of women and children (al-Bukhārī 3015).
- Abū Bakr instructed Usāma's army not to kill children, the elderly or women; not to cut down fruit trees; not to slaughter livestock except for food; and to leave monks in their cells undisturbed.
- Treaties must be honoured; treachery is forbidden.
- Force must be proportionate: "do not transgress" (2:190).

### The mainstream view of violence today

Mainstream scholarship overwhelmingly holds that self-declared "jihad" by non-state actors, suicide attacks and the targeting of civilians are forbidden and are a corruption of the doctrine. This has been stated repeatedly in collective declarations such as the Amman Message (2005) and the Marrakesh Declaration (2016).

## 3. The Ahmadi position

Ahmadis hold that the *conditions* under which armed jihad was permitted (the violent suppression of the freedom to believe and worship) did not exist in Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's time, and that the task of the Messianic age is the defence of Islam by argument. He taught that the jihad of the sword, in the sense of religious warfare, had ended for his age, and that its obligation is *jihad bil-qalam*, the jihad of the pen. The position is set out principally in *Government Angrezi aur Jihad* (1900).

### The Quranic basis

> **25:52**: "So obey not the disbelievers, and **strive against them by means of it** [the Quran] **a great striving**" (*jāhidhum bihī jihādan kabīrā*).

Ahmadis regard this as the key verse. It is Meccan; the instrument of striving is named explicitly as the Quran; and the striving is called *kabīr*, "great". The great jihad, on this reading, is argument from revelation. They also cite:

> **2:256**: "There is no compulsion in religion."
>
> **16:125**: "Call unto the way of thy Lord with wisdom and goodly exhortation, and argue with them in a way that is best."
>
> **29:69**: "And as for those who strive in Our path, We will surely guide them in Our ways."

Ahmadis distinguish three levels: the greatest jihad, against one's own self; the "great jihad" with the Quran (25:52); and the lesser jihad of fighting. The Messiah's age, they say, is the age of jihad by the pen. They also cite 47:4, which speaks of fighting "until war lays down its burdens".

### The argument from the Messiah's own description

In the hadith of the Messiah's descent, the standard text of al-Bukhārī (3448) says he will "break the cross, kill the swine and abolish the jizya". A variant reading of the same hadith, which Ahmadis cite, says he will "lay down war" (*yaḍaʿu l-ḥarb*). On the Ahmadi reading, the hallmark of the Messianic age is the laying down of arms, and the "breaking of the cross" is achieved by refutation rather than force: the Messiah's weapons are stated in the prophecy itself.

**Mainstream reply:** the end of war describes the state of affairs *after* the Messiah has defeated the Dajjal and all people have accepted the truth. It is an outcome, not a prohibition in advance.

### The "greater jihad"

Ahmadis, like many Sufi traditions, emphasise the struggle against the self as the primary and permanent jihad. The frequently quoted saying "we have returned from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad" is, in fairness, **graded weak or baseless by most hadith critics**, including al-Bayhaqī and Ibn Taymiyya, and it is better not to rest an argument on it.

The underlying idea does have sound support:

> "The *mujāhid* is one who strives against his own soul in obedience to Allah."
> (al-Tirmidhī 1621, graded authentic by a number of scholars)

## 4. What both communities hold in common

- Terrorism is forbidden.
- Killing non-combatants (women, children, the elderly, clergy) is forbidden.
- Suicide attacks are forbidden.
- Forced conversion is forbidden (2:256).
- Treachery and the violation of treaties are forbidden.
- Striving against one's own soul is a permanent obligation.
- Private individuals and groups may not declare war.

# Part Two: The colonial context

## 5. The historical setting

- **Sikh rule in the Punjab (c. 1799–1849).** Muslim accounts, including Ahmadi ones, describe restrictions on Muslim worship in this period; in Lahore, the Badshahi Mosque was used by Sikh forces as a stable and arsenal. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's family lost its estates in the early Sikh period and partly recovered them under Ranjit Singh.
- **British annexation (1849) and the 1857 uprising.** After 1857 the British regarded Muslims with particular suspicion. W. W. Hunter's *The Indian Musalmans* (1871) asked whether Muslims were bound in conscience to rebel against the Queen, and followers of the earlier jihad movement of Sayyid Ahmad of Rae Bareli were prosecuted in the "Wahhabi trials" of the 1860s and 1870s.
- **Muslim leaders' response.** Many leading Muslims argued that armed jihad against British rule was not valid. They included Sir Syed Ahmad Khan; Ahl-e-Hadith figures such as Sayyid Nazir Husain Dehlvi, Nawab Siddiq Hasan Khan (*Tarjuman-e-Wahhabiyyat*) and Muhammad Husain Batalvi (whose treatise on jihad declared it unlawful against the British); and the authors of fatwas that Hunter himself quoted. Deobandi elders such as Rashid Ahmad Gangohi also declared their loyalty.
- **The real battleground was religious debate.** Late-19th-century Punjab saw intense printed polemics between Christian missionaries, the Hindu reform movement Arya Samaj, and Muslim scholars. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's career was largely a campaign against Christian and Arya Samaj attacks on Islam.

## 6. What Mirza Ghulam Ahmad said and did

- **His family's service.** He wrote with pride that in 1857 his father, Mirza Ghulam Murtaza, supplied the British with fifty horses and fifty mounted men, and that his brother served as well. He reprinted British officials' letters praising the family, for example in *Kashf-ul-Ghita* ("The Truth Unveiled", 1898), a treatise addressed to the government.
- **Praise of British rule.** His books repeatedly praise the government's justice and religious freedom and pray for it. He wrote *Tuhfa-e-Qaisariya* (1897) for Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee and *Sitara-e-Qaisara* (1899); both also invited the Queen to accept Islam.
- **Against armed jihad.** *Government Angrezi aur Jihad* ("The British Government and Jihad", 22 May 1900) argues that the Prophet's wars were defensive; that the idea of jihad had been distorted, partly, he said, by missionary books portraying Islam as a religion of the sword; and that everyone who takes the bai'at must accept that religious war is forbidden in this age, because the Messiah has come. A well-known Urdu poem of his from this period begins (in translation): "Now give up the thought of jihad, my friends; for religion, war and killing are now forbidden."
- **Distribution.** In *Sitara-e-Qaisara* he said he had published around fifty thousand books, journals and posters, distributed in India and in Muslim countries, promoting loyalty and his view of jihad. Critics also often quote a passage in *Tiryaq-ul-Qulub* saying that his writings in support of the government and against jihad would fill fifty cupboards.
- **"Self-planted sapling" (*khud-kāshta paudā*).** In a petition to the Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab (1898) he asked the government to treat "this self-planted sapling" with care and consideration: a family that had proved its loyalty over some fifty years.

## 7. The Ahmadi explanation

1. **What Islamic law requires.** The Quran permits fighting for those who are attacked and driven from their homes "only because they say, 'Our Lord is Allah'" (22:39–40). Fighting is against those who fight you, without going beyond limits (2:190); there is no compulsion in religion (2:256); and if the enemy inclines to peace, Muslims must too (8:61). The British did not fight Muslims because of their religion. Muslims could pray, preach, build mosques and give the azan freely. So the conditions for religious war were absent.
2. **Suspended, not abolished.** The claim is not that 2:190 or 22:39 was cancelled, but that the *condition* those verses specify was not present. A conditional permission whose condition is absent is dormant, not abolished. He taught that armed jihad did not apply *in the conditions of his time*, not that defensive war was abolished for ever. Ahmadis point to the **Furqan Battalion**, a volunteer force of Ahmadis raised in June 1948 at the Pakistan government's request. It fought in the first Kashmir war until it was disbanded in June 1950 and was praised by the army's commander-in-chief.
3. **Obedience to the state is a separate principle.** The duty not to rebel against established authority is independent of the jihad question, and is grounded in 4:59 ("obey those in authority among you") and in the hadith forbidding rebellion. Rebellion would have been both unlawful and a breach of the covenant of citizenship.
4. **It was aimed at misuse.** His writings on the subject are directed at those who invoked jihad for plunder or for violence against non-combatants, and he argued that such conduct had defamed Islam.
5. **The teaching cuts against the community's own interests too.** The same position forbids Ahmadis armed resistance against the states that have persecuted them. It has not been applied selectively in their favour.
6. **He said what other scholars said.** Ahmadi compilations, especially from the Lahore branch, collect statements of loyalty and against rebellion from Sir Syed, Nazir Husain Dehlvi, Batalvi, Siddiq Hasan Khan, Deobandi elders and others. They also note that Batalvi himself reported Mirza Ghulam Ahmad to the government as potentially seditious, comparing him to the Mahdi of Sudan.
7. **Many loyalty statements were defensive.** A good part of this literature answered accusations of sedition made by his opponents; he complained that Muslims in government service were trying to paint him as a rebel.
8. **He was no favoured client.** In 1897 he was tried on a charge, brought by the missionary Dr Henry Martyn Clark (with Batalvi appearing for the prosecution), of plotting murder; the District Magistrate, Captain M. W. Douglas, dismissed it. He faced other court cases, and the police searched his house after Lekh Ram's murder.
9. **He attacked the rulers' religion.** His central mission, proving that Jesus died and refuting the Trinity and the atonement, struck at the faith of the ruling power, and he invited the Queen herself to Islam.
10. **"Self-planted sapling" referred to his family's record**, not to the movement or his claim, which he always attributed to God.
11. **Later political involvement.** The second Caliph, Mirza Bashiruddin Mahmud Ahmad, chaired the All-India Kashmir Committee (1931), which campaigned for Kashmiri Muslims. Sir Muhammad Zafarullah Khan presided over the All-India Muslim League in 1931, prepared the Muslim League's case before the Punjab Boundary Commission in 1947, and as Pakistan's first Foreign Minister argued its case on Kashmir at the United Nations.

## 8. The critics' case

1. **The language goes far beyond what religious freedom required.** Critics point to repeated, effusive praise of the Queen and the government, his family's support for the British in 1857, and the "fifty cupboards" passage. They argue that no other religious leader made loyalty so central to his message.
2. **Abrogation, not legal opinion.** Other scholars gave a legal judgment that armed jihad was not valid *in present conditions*. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, as Messiah, declared it forbidden for his age, made accepting this part of the bai'at, and dismissed the Mahdi of Muslim expectation as a "bloody Mahdi". Critics see this as using prophetic authority to remove an Islamic obligation at exactly the moment it suited the colonial power.
3. **Only revelation can qualify revelation.** A standing Quranic permission cannot be suspended for an era on any human authority. The judgement that an era of the sword has ended is itself a claim to prophetic authority, which returns the argument to Part 1 (the finality of prophethood).
4. **The conditions were not absent everywhere.** The conditions in 22:39 were plainly present elsewhere in the Muslim world at the time, so a judgement that they were absent could not be universal.
5. **The "plant" phrase.** Critics read the 1898 petition as presenting himself and his community, not just his ancestors, as a British creation.
6. **Exporting the message.** His anti-jihad writings were sent to Arab lands, Afghanistan and Turkey, beyond British India, where, critics argue, they could only serve imperial interests.
7. **The nationalist critique.** In the 1930s the anti-colonial Majlis-e-Ahrar and others attacked Ahmadis as collaborators. Muhammad Iqbal argued in 1935 that their claim to prophethood split Muslim unity, and urged the British government to declare them a separate community. This "collaborator" charge later fed the 1953 and 1974 campaigns in Pakistan (Part 4).

**Ahmadi counter to point 3:** the question of *who* makes the judgement is indeed downstream of the prophethood question, but the *substance* (that argument is the right instrument in this age) can be assessed on the Quranic evidence independently.

## 9. The Lahore branch

The Lahore branch shares the same defence, often in more detail; its websites hold large collections of comparable statements by other Muslim leaders of the period. It stresses that his mission was religious reform, not politics.

## 10. How historians frame it

Historians of the period, such as Spencer Lavan and Adil Hussain Khan, generally read his statements against the background of widespread Muslim accommodation with British rule after 1857, while noting that tying the end of armed jihad to his own messianic claim was distinctive to him.

## 11. The disagreement stated precisely

> **Can the permissibility of defensive *qitāl* be suspended for an era by the judgement of a claimed Messiah, or does it remain a standing provision whose conditions may simply not apply at a given time?**

And on the history:

> **Was his loyalty to British rule the ordinary gratitude of a religious minority for freedom of worship, or political accommodation dressed in religious terms?**

Notice how little separates the two positions in practice. A mainstream scholar who holds that the conditions for armed jihad are not currently met, and an Ahmadi who holds that the era of the sword has ended, will give the same answer to almost every practical question. The difference is in the basis, not usually the outcome.

## 12. Summary

**The meaning of jihad**

| | Mainstream | Ahmadi |
|---|---|---|
| Is jihad primarily war? | No; it is striving in several forms | No; it is striving in several forms |
| Is *qitāl* a valid category? | Yes, under strict conditions | Yes in principle; its conditions were absent in his era |
| Who may declare it? | Legitimate state authority | Not applicable in the present age |
| Primary jihad today | Struggle against the self; defence when conditions arise | *Jihad bil-qalam*, the pen |
| Key verse | 22:39–40 | 25:52 |
| Terrorism | Forbidden | Forbidden |

**The colonial question**

| Issue | Critics | Ahmadi answer |
|---|---|---|
| Praise of British rule | Excessive and self-serving | Gratitude for religious freedom after Sikh rule; common among Muslim leaders of the time |
| His family in 1857 | Sided with the British against Muslims | A matter of record that he cited to answer charges of sedition |
| Ending armed jihad | A messianic abrogation that suited the empire | The conditions for religious war were absent; the Messiah's jihad is by argument; defensive war remains valid |
| "Self-planted sapling" | An admission of British sponsorship | Referred to his family's long record, not his mission |
| Relationship with the British | Collaboration | He was prosecuted and investigated, and spent his life refuting the rulers' religion |

## Practical note

When this comes up, lead with the common ground, because most people assume the disagreement is far wider than it is. Then narrow it to the single real question: not *whether* armed jihad exists as a category, but whether its enabling conditions apply now, and who has the authority to judge that.

Do not use the "greater jihad" hadith as a proof text. Its grading is genuinely weak, and an opponent who knows that will use it to discredit stronger arguments made alongside it. 25:52 does the same work and is Quran.
