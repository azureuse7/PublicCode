# 2. The Interpretation of the End-Times Prophecies

*Part 2 of 6 in a series on the main points of dispute between Ahmadi Muslims and mainstream Muslims: the Mahdi and Messiah narrations, whether they describe one person or two, the signs claimed as fulfilled, and the question of how prophecy should be read. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's own disputed prophecies are covered in Part 5, and the descent hadith in Part 3.*

---

## The shape of the disagreement

This topic is less about a single verse than about **method**: how prophetic language should be read. Mainstream scholarship reads the end-times narrations as describing future events in substantially literal terms. Ahmadis read much of the same material symbolically, and hold that it has already been fulfilled.

The argument therefore has two layers:

1. **Content** — do the texts describe one figure or two? What do the signs mean?
2. **Method** — when is a prophecy to be read literally, and when symbolically?

The second layer is usually where the real disagreement lives, and it is worth getting to quickly.

---

## Part One: One figure or two?

### The mainstream position

The Mahdi and the Messiah are **two distinct persons**:

- **The Mahdi** is a descendant of the Prophet ﷺ through Fatimah, appearing at the end of time to lead the Muslims. The narrations are found in **Abu Dawud 4282–4290**, **Tirmidhi 2230–2232**, and **Ibn Majah 4082–4088**. The most cited: "Even if only one day remained of this world, Allah would lengthen that day until He sent a man from my family whose name is like my name and whose father's name is like my father's name."
- **Jesus (as)** then descends and **prays behind** the Mahdi. Mainstream scholars regard this detail as theologically significant: a prophet praying behind a member of this ummah demonstrates that the sharia of Muhammad ﷺ is supreme and that no new law is being brought.

This yields a concrete objection to the Ahmadi claim: the Mahdi is described as an Arab of the Prophet's ﷺ lineage, named Muhammad son of Abdullah. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad was a Punjabi of Mughal descent named Ghulam Ahmad son of Ghulam Murtaza.

### The Ahmadiyya position

The Mahdi and the Messiah are **one person**, and that person arises from within the ummah.

**The central argument** is the hadith both communities accept as authentic:

> "How will you be when the son of Mary descends among you **and your imam is from among you** (*wa imāmukum minkum*)?"
> — **Bukhari 3449, Muslim 155**

Ahmadis argue the most natural reading is that the descending Messiah *is* the imam from among you — that the clause explains who he will be, not who he will stand behind. On the mainstream reading, they contend, the phrase requires importing a second character the sentence does not mention.

Ahmadis also cite:

> "There is no Mahdi except Jesus son of Mary."
> — **Ibn Majah 4039**

**This should be used carefully.** The report is graded weak by most hadith critics, including Ibn Majah's own commentators. Ahmadis who rely on it should acknowledge the grading; the *imāmukum minkum* argument is far stronger and does not depend on it.

**On the lineage objection:** Ahmadis respond that the narrations specifying the Mahdi's descent and name are themselves contested in grading and conflict with each other in detail; that "a name like my name" can be satisfied by *Ahmad* (**Quran 61:6**, where Jesus foretells a messenger named Ahmad); and that the Promised Messiah's family traced Persian and Mughal descent, with spiritual rather than genealogical kinship being the operative sense.

**Mainstream rejoinder:** the Mahdi narrations, while individually varying, are numerous enough that the core description is not easily set aside, and reinterpreting both the lineage and the name is a great deal of reinterpretation to carry.

## Part Two: The signs claimed as fulfilled

### The eclipses of Ramadan

**The report.** *Sunan al-Daraqutni* records, attributed to Imam Muhammad al-Baqir:

> For our Mahdi there are two signs which have never appeared since the creation of the heavens and the earth: the moon will be eclipsed on the first of the nights of [lunar] eclipse in Ramadan, and the sun will be eclipsed in the middle of the days on which solar eclipses occur in Ramadan.

**The Ahmadi claim.** Lunar eclipses in Ramadan can only fall on the 13th, 14th or 15th of the lunar month, and solar eclipses only on the 27th, 28th or 29th. The report therefore specifies the *first* of the possible lunar dates (13th) and the *middle* of the possible solar dates (28th). Ahmadis hold that in Ramadan 1311 AH (March 1894) a lunar eclipse occurred on the 13th and a solar eclipse on the 28th, visible in the East, and that the pattern repeated the following year visible in the West — during the lifetime of a claimant to the office of Mahdi, which Ahmadis consider the essential element.

**The mainstream reply**, on three grounds:

1. **Grading.** The chain is criticised; it passes through narrators including Jabir al-Juʿfi and ʿAmr ibn Shamir, both heavily impugned by hadith critics. Many scholars classify the report as very weak or fabricated. A sign cannot be stronger than the text that predicts it.
2. **Astronomy.** Eclipses falling on those dates in Ramadan are not unique; the pattern recurs at intervals, so the report's claim that it has never happened since creation is factually difficult.
3. **Logic.** Even granting the sign, it establishes only that an astronomical event occurred during someone's claim — not that the claim was true.

**Ahmadi rejoinder:** the sign is cited as corroboration within a wider case, not as a standalone proof, and the specificity of the dating is still striking.

*An honest presentation concedes the grading problem openly. Defending a weak chain damages the credibility of stronger arguments made alongside it.*

### The plague

The plague epidemic in Punjab from 1897 onward is cited against narrations of pestilence in the Messiah's time, and against the Promised Messiah's own published prophecy of a sign of divine chastisement. Ahmadis also connect **Quran 27:82** (the *dābbat al-arḍ*) to this period.

**Mainstream reply:** epidemics are historically common and this one was regional; the connection is interpretive rather than evidential.

### The abandonment of the camel

> **Quran 81:4** — "And when the she-camels, ten months with young, are abandoned."
>
> And from the descent narrations: the camel shall be abandoned and not used.

Ahmadis read this as fulfilled by railways, steamships and motor transport displacing the caravan — including on the routes to the Hajj.

**Mainstream reply:** this is a plausible reading, and many mainstream commentators also read 81:4 as referring to modern transport. It does not by itself identify a particular claimant.

### The comet

Ahmadis cite the appearance of a comet during the period, linked to a report that a comet appeared in the time of Yahya (John the Baptist), the Great Comet of 1882 being the usual reference.

### The Dajjal and Gog and Magog

Ahmadis read:

- **The Dajjal** as the civilisation of materialism combined with organised Christian missionary power — the "one-eyed" description taken as seeing only the material and not the spiritual
- **Gog and Magog** as the dominant global powers of the modern age

**Mainstream reply:** the sahih narrations describe the Dajjal with concrete physical particulars — blind in one eye, with *kāfir* written between his eyes, performing specific deeds over a defined period — which read as a description of an individual rather than a civilisation.

## Part Three: The method question

This is the heart of it, and the most productive place to argue.

### The Ahmadi case for symbolic reading

Ahmadis argue that symbolic fulfilment of prophecy is a scriptural norm, not a convenient escape:

**Within the Quran.** Yusuf's dream of eleven stars, the sun and the moon prostrating (**12:4**) was fulfilled in his family's arrival, and the Quran itself calls this the interpretation of the vision (**12:100**). The dream was true; it was simply not literal.

**In the Prophet's ﷺ practice.** He interpreted dreams symbolically as a matter of course — milk as knowledge, a shirt as religion.

**The strongest analogy: Elijah and John the Baptist.** Jewish expectation held that Elijah would return bodily before the Messiah (**Malachi 4:5**). In the Gospels, Jesus states that John the Baptist is the Elijah who was to come (**Matthew 11:14, 17:12–13**) — while John, asked directly whether he was Elijah, said he was not (**John 1:21**).

Ahmadis consider this decisive by analogy: a prophecy of a named individual's return was fulfilled by a *different person coming in his spirit and power*, and the generation that insisted on literal fulfilment rejected the fulfilment when it came. They argue the same structure applies to the return of the Messiah.

**Mainstream reply:** the analogy is drawn from scripture that Muslims hold to be textually unreliable, and in any case an argument from Christian precedent cannot override explicit Islamic texts. Additionally, there is no equivalent in the Islamic sources to Jesus's statement identifying the fulfilment — the identification in the Ahmadi case is made by the claimant himself.

### The mainstream case for literal reading

- The narrations contain physical particulars — a location (east of Damascus), clothing, the manner of descent, specific acts — that are not the language of symbol
- The early community and fourteen centuries of scholarship understood them literally
- Symbolic interpretation is unfalsifiable: any event can be read as fulfilling a sufficiently flexible symbol, and signs that cannot fail cannot confirm

**Ahmadi counter:** literalism has its own failure mode — it renders prophecy permanently unfulfillable, since any claimant can be rejected for not having descended from the sky.

---

## The disagreement stated precisely

> **Do these prophecies await a future, physical fulfilment, or were they fulfilled spiritually at the end of the 19th century?**

And beneath it:

> **What kind of language is prophecy, and who decides when it has been fulfilled?**

## Practical note

Three things will make this discussion go better:

**Lead with *imāmukum minkum*, not the eclipses.** It is a sahih hadith in both Bukhari and Muslim, it goes directly to the one-or-two-persons question, and it does not require anyone to accept a contested chain first.

**Concede the weak reports openly.** The Daraqutni eclipse narration and "there is no Mahdi but Jesus" (Ibn Majah 4039) are both genuinely weak. Saying so costs little and buys a great deal of credibility for the arguments that are strong.

**Make the method argument explicitly.** Most of these conversations are really a disagreement about how prophecy works, conducted as though it were a disagreement about facts. Naming that — and putting the Elijah/John the Baptist structure on the table — moves it forward faster than trading signs.
