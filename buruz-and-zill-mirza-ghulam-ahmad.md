# Burūz (بروز) and Ẓill (ظل) in the Thought of Mirza Ghulam Ahmad

*An explanatory overview, including how the concept is understood inside the Ahmadiyya movement and how it is criticised outside it.*

---

## 1. Summary

**Burūz** ("manifestation, appearance") and **ẓill** ("shadow, reflection") are two closely related terms that Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (1835–1908, Qadian, Punjab) used to explain his claims to be:

- the **Promised Messiah** and **Mahdi** whom Muslims were awaiting, and
- a **prophet (nabī) subordinate to Muhammad**, whose prophethood is a *reflection* of Muhammad's rather than a separate one.

The core idea: a person can so completely absorb the qualities of a prophet that he becomes that prophet's "second appearance" (*burūz*), or a "shadow" (*ẓill*) of him. Whatever such a person has, he has *only* through the original. He adds nothing, brings no new law, and does not compete with the original.

This is the most contested part of Ahmadiyya doctrine, because it bears directly on the Islamic belief in the **finality of prophethood (khatm-e-nubuwwat)**.

---

## 2. The words

| Term | Literal meaning | Sense in this usage |
|---|---|---|
| **Ẓill** | shadow, image, reflection | A person whose status is a reflection of another's. A shadow has no independent existence. |
| **Burūz** | coming forth, appearing, manifestation | A person in whom the qualities of an earlier figure re-appear, like a second appearance of that figure. |
| **Majāzī** | metaphorical | Used in the Lahore Ahmadiyya reading (see §7). |
| **Juz'ī** | partial | A share of, not the whole of, prophethood. |

A common illustration is the moon and the sun: the moon shines with borrowed light, yet in a sense "is" the sun's light to us at night. Ahmad also used the image of a mirror in which the Prophet's qualities are reflected.

---

## 3. Background: where the vocabulary comes from

Ahmad did not invent these words. They belong to the vocabulary of Islamic mysticism (Sufism), where:

- **Burūz / ẓill** describe how a saint can reflect the spiritual state of a prophet. Sufi writers commonly say that a saint's perfections are "shadows" of the perfections of prophethood.
- **Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi** (1564–1624, the *Mujaddid Alf-i Thānī*) wrote of sainthood as the shadow of prophethood. Ahmadis cite him as precedent.
- **Ibn ʿArabī** (d. 1240) is often cited by Ahmadis for the view that *law-bearing* prophethood ended with Muhammad while a wider spiritual "general" prophethood or sainthood continues.

**How the two sides read this precedent**

- *Ahmadi view:* these authorities show that "shadow" and "manifestation" are established Islamic categories, so Ahmad's usage isn't a novelty.
- *Critics' view:* the earlier writers applied these terms to **saints (awliyā')**, not to prophets. Nobody in that tradition claimed that a shadow of a prophet is himself a *nabī*.

---

## 4. How Ahmad used the concepts

### 4.1 Burūz of the Messiah (Jesus)

Ahmad argued that the Messiah expected at the end of time would not be the same Jesus returning in the flesh but a **person who comes in Jesus's "spirit and power"**, like the way John the Baptist was seen as the "Elijah" who was to come. Because a *burūz* shares the qualities of the original, he can carry the original's *name and title*. This is how Ahmad claimed to be "the Messiah" while remaining a distinct person.

### 4.2 Burūz of Muhammad

The more far-reaching step. Ahmad claimed to be a **burūz of Muhammad**, meaning Muhammad's qualities and mission re-appearing in him, through complete devotion. He linked this to Qur'an 62:3 ("others among them who have not yet joined them"), which Ahmadis read as prophesying a second appearance of the Prophet's spiritual influence in a later age.

He said that on this basis, God had given him the names **Muhammad** and **Ahmad**, and that he was thus not a separate claimant but the Prophet's own reflection.

### 4.3 Ẓillī nabī: a "shadow prophet"

Ahmad said that God addressed him as *nabī* and *rasūl* in revelations. His explanation:

- He is **not** a prophet who brings a **new law (sharīʿa)**.
- He is **not** an **independent (mustaqill)** prophet who stands apart from Muhammad.
- He **is** a *nabī* only **"by way of ẓill"**, through perfect obedience to Muhammad, receiving Muhammad's name and prophetic attributes by reflection.

Ahmad's argument is that a shadow doesn't detract from the person casting it, so Muhammad's status as **Khātam an-Nabiyyīn** ("Seal of the Prophets") is left intact. He argued that what would contradict the seal is an *independent* prophet, not a follower who receives everything through Muhammad. Ahmadis also often interpret "seal" as the seal of *authentication* (only those bearing Muhammad's stamp can be prophets) rather than the *end* of prophecy.

**Consequence within this framework:** the door to *ẓillī* prophethood is understood by Ahmadis to have opened through Ahmad. Within Qadiani-Ahmadi teaching, it is not a station that others claim after him. Instead, the caliphate continues his mission.

### 4.4 A three-part classification (as Ahmadis present it)

1. **Law-bearing prophets** (bring a new *sharīʿa*): ended with Muhammad.
2. **Independent prophets without a new law:** also closed.
3. **Ẓillī / ummatī prophets** (follower prophets who receive revelation solely through the Prophet): remains possible.

## 5. Key texts (for anyone wanting to read the originals)

Approximate order of relevance. I have not verified page numbers, so treat this as a starting list.

- ***Barāhīn-e-Aḥmadiyya*** (1880–84; Part 5, 1905): early revelations, including being addressed with titles later interpreted as *burūzī*.
- ***Izāla-e-Auhām*** (1891): often cited by critics as a place where he denies claiming prophethood and describes himself as a *muḥaddath* (one spoken to by God).
- ***Ek Ghalaṭī kā Izāla*** ("Correction of a Mistake", 1901): a central text where he explains his relationship to the title *nabī* via *ẓill*.
- ***Nuzūl-ul-Masīḥ*** (1902): includes the clarification that he is a *nabī* and *rasūl* only in the sense of perfect *ẓilliyyat*, not with a new law or new name.
- ***Ḥaqīqat-ul-Waḥy*** (1907): includes the statement that he is a *ẓillī nabī* by virtue of perfect obedience.
- ***Tadhkirah***: the compilation of his revelations, dreams and visions.

---

## 6. The timeline question

The biggest scholarly and polemical question is **whether his position changed over time.**

- **Ahmadi (Qadiani/mainstream) view:** His understanding *deepened*. Early on he applied the title *nabī* to himself in a loose sense and considered himself a *muḥaddath*. As the frequency and content of revelations addressing him as *nabī* and *rasūl* accumulated, he concluded that he was in fact a *nabī*, of the *ẓillī/burūzī* type. His 1901 writings mark an explicit correction of his earlier view.
- **Critics' view:** the change shows inconsistency: earlier denials of prophethood versus later claims. They argue the *ẓillī* framework was developed to accommodate a claim that would otherwise be rejected.
- **Lahore Ahmadiyya view:** there was no change. His consistent position was that he was not a prophet in any real sense (see §7).

---

## 7. The internal Ahmadiyya split

The concept is one of the doctrinal reasons the community divided in **1914**.

| | **Qadiani / mainstream Ahmadiyya** | **Lahore Ahmadiyya** |
|---|---|---|
| Was Ahmad a prophet? | Yes, a *ẓillī, ummatī* prophet: really a *nabī*, though subordinate | No: a **mujaddid** (reformer), **Messiah** and **Mahdi**, not a prophet |
| Meaning of *ẓill/burūz* | A true, though derivative, prophethood | Metaphorical: the appearance of *some qualities* of prophethood in a non-prophet |
| Terms used for the status | Ẓillī, burūzī nabī | Ẓillī, burūzī, *majāzī*, *juz'ī*, *nāqiṣ* ("imperfect") prophethood, meaning not real prophethood |
| Reading of his statements | Statements denying prophethood refer to law-bearing/independent prophethood | Statements *affirming* the title *nabī* are metaphorical/literal-linguistic, and the denials are the true position |

The Lahore side supports its reading with an argument from language: the Prophet Muhammad himself is reported to have called a ruler "the *ẓill* of God", yet nobody concludes a king is divine, so a "shadow prophet" is likewise not a prophet.

Both branches use the same vocabulary but reach opposite conclusions about what it implies.

---

## 8. Criticism from mainstream Muslim scholarship

Most Sunni and Shia scholars reject the concept as applied by Ahmad. The main objections:

1. **Consensus on finality.** The overwhelming majority position holds that *no* kind of prophethood (independent, follower, law-bearing or not, shadow or literal) can follow Muhammad. They read *Khātam an-Nabiyyīn* as "the last of the prophets".
2. **Category error.** *Ẓill* and *burūz* were used in earlier tradition for saints. Calling a saint the "shadow" of a prophet never meant he *was* a prophet, and hence gives no precedent for a "shadow prophet."
3. **Rebranding.** Critics argue that if a *ẓillī nabī* receives revelation from God, is addressed as *nabī* and *rasūl*, and must be believed in, then he functions as a prophet whatever the label.
4. **Transmigration.** Some polemicists say *burūz* amounts to *tanāsukh* (reincarnation). Ahmadis reject this firmly. They say *burūz* describes spiritual resemblance and continuation of a mission, not the transfer of a soul.
5. **Consistency.** Objections about changing statements over time (§6).

**Legal and social consequences.** These theological disputes have had serious real-world effects. In **Pakistan**, the 1974 constitutional amendment declared Ahmadis non-Muslims, and the 1984 ordinance (Ordinance XX) criminalised various Ahmadi religious practices. Ahmadis consider themselves Muslims and face persecution in several countries as a result. I mention this so the philosophical debate isn't mistaken for a purely academic one.

---

## 9. How to read this fairly

- **Terms:** *burūz* and *ẓill* are real Sufi terms, so the vocabulary is not made up.
- **The disputed step:** whether that vocabulary can extend from *sainthood* to *prophethood*. Ahmad and most Ahmadis say yes. Most other Muslims say no.
- **Even inside the movement:** the two branches read the same texts differently, which shows how much interpretation is involved.
- **Sources:** much of what is easily found online for this topic is written by partisans of one side or another (Qadiani, Lahori, or critics). If you are studying this seriously, read Ahmad's own texts and academic works (e.g. by scholars of South Asian Islam) alongside the advocacy material.
