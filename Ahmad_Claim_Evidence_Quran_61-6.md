# Who is "Ahmad" in Qur'an 61:6? — The Ahmadiyya Case and Its Evidential Weight

**Subject:** The Ahmadiyya Muslim Community's claim that the "Ahmad" foretold in Sūrah al-Ṣaff 61:6 is Mirza Ghulam Ahmad of Qadian (1835–1908).

**Primary source for the argument:** *Anwār-ul-Khilāfat* by Hazrat Mirza Bashir-ud-Din Mahmud Ahmad, Khalifatul Masih II — addresses delivered at the Jalsa Salana, Qadian, 27, 28 and 30 December 1915; first published 1916. Page references below are to the printed pages of the 2016 Qadian edition (Nazarat Nashr-o-Isha'at, ISBN 978-81-7912-142-9), as translated in the companion file `Anwar-ul-Khilafat_pages_13-53_English.md`.

---

## The verse

> **61:6** — "And when Jesus, son of Mary, said: O Children of Israel, I am indeed Allah's messenger to you, confirming the Torah that came before me and giving glad tidings of a messenger to come after me, whose name is Ahmad. But when he came to them with clear proofs, they said: this is plain sorcery."

> **61:7** — "And who is more unjust than one who forges a lie against Allah while he is being called to Islam? And Allah guides not the wrongdoing people."

**Numbering note.** Ahmadiyya editions count the *Bismillāh* as verse 1, so their citations run one higher than standard (Kufan) numbering. Their "al-Ṣaff: 7" and "al-Ṣaff: 8" are standard 61:6 and 61:7. This document uses standard numbering.

## The claim, stated precisely

The Ahmadiyya Muslim Community (Qadian/Rabwah branch) holds that the primary referent is **Mirza Ghulam Ahmad**, not the Prophet Muhammad. The claim is framed as a **name-versus-attribute** distinction rather than a denial:

- The Prophet Muhammad *was* Ahmad — Ahmad was his *ṣifah* (attribute). Mahmud Ahmad states that anyone denying this "speaks falsely" and "cannot be a believer at all."
- But Ahmad was not his *ism* (personal name), which was Muhammad.
- The prophecy specifies a *name*, and therefore points to a later person.

The doctrine holding this together is **burūz / ẓill** (manifestation / shadow): Ghulam Ahmad claimed to be not an independent prophet but a spiritual reflection and second advent of Muhammad. On this view the prophecy still lands on Muhammad — through his reflection.

**Important:** the Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement (Muhammad Ali, Khwaja Kamal-ud-Din) rejects this and holds Ahmad = the Prophet Muhammad, in line with mainstream Islam. The 1915 addresses are a polemic *against* that faction, delivered a year after the 1914 split.

# The Evidence, as the movement presents it

## Strand 1 — The negative case: the Prophet's name was not Ahmad

*(pp. 13–29)*

| Evidence | Claim |
|---|---|
| The Qur'an | Names him **Muhammad** (4×), never Ahmad |
| *Kalimah*, *adhān*, *takbīr*, *durūd* | All use Muhammad; "Ahmad-ur-Rasūlullāh" is nowhere permitted |
| His letters and seal | Sealed with Muhammad; never signed Ahmad |
| Companions' speech | No narration shows any Companion addressing him as Ahmad |
| Ibn Hishām 1:62 | His mother was told in a dream *sammihi Muhammadan* — "name him Muhammad" |

**On the "I have several names" hadith** (Bukhārī / Mishkāt — Muhammad, Ahmad, Māḥī, Ḥāshir, ʿĀqib): the reply is that Arabic *ism* also means *attribute* — the Qur'an calls God's attributes *asmā'*, though His only personal name is Allah. Since Māḥī, Ḥāshir and ʿĀqib are universally read as attributes, consistency requires the same for Ahmad. Reinforced by the Musnad Aḥmad version (4:395), which adds *al-Muqaffī*, *Nabī al-Raḥmah*, *Nabī al-Tawbah*, *Nabī al-Malḥamah* — titles nobody treats as names, and which pagan Arabs could not have conferred at birth.

**On the narration that his mother was told "Ahmad":** dismissed as fabricated, its transmitter (Wāqidī and "others of that stamp") being *munkar al-ḥadīth*.

**On the Paraclete:** rather than conceding it, the argument claims it for the Prophet anyway — deriving *Fāraqlīṭ* from *fāriq* + *līṭ*, "one who puts falsehood/Satan to flight," so no rival prophecy is lost. It further cites Nawab Siddiq Hasan Khan's *Fatḥ al-Bayān* (9:335) that the Gospel of Barnabas names the Prophet **Muhammad** — arguing that if the Gospel says Muhammad, the "Ahmad" prophecy must be a separate one.

## Strand 2 — Ten proofs that Ghulam Ahmad's name was "Ahmad"

*(pp. 30–38)*

1. **Toponymic** — his father founded a village named **Ahmadabad** after him, not Ghulam-Ahmadabad; his brother Ghulam Qadir's village is **Qadirabad**. Inference: "Ghulam" was a family prefix (father Ghulām Murtaḍā; uncles Ghulām Ḥaidar, Ghulām Muḥyī-ud-Dīn), not part of the name.
2. **Grammatical** — "Ghulam Ahmad" is not a valid compound in any language: Arabic would require *Ghulāmu Aḥmada*, Persian *Ghulām-e-Aḥmad*, Punjabi *Aḥmad dā ghulām*. None matches.
3. He attached "Ahmad" to all his sons' names as a family mark.
4. He administered the *bai'at* in the name "Ahmad" — Mahmud Ahmad calls the live audience as witness and records their shouted confirmation.
5. He signed several of his books simply "**Ahmad**."
6. His revelations address him as Ahmad abundantly.
7. **Khalifa I's testimony** in *Mabādī-uṣ-Ṣarf wan-Naḥw*: "Ahmad is the name of this Imam of ours who appeared from Qadian."
8. **The opponents' own usage** — Muhammad Ali titled his biography of Ghulam Ahmad *Ahmad*; and the Lahore Anjuman's published *bai'at* formula reads: "at the hand of Muhammad Ali, I enter into the *bai'at* of **Ahmad**."
9. He declared himself the fulfilment — *Izāla-e-Awhām* 2:673, arguing Muhammad is a name of *jalāl* (majesty) and Ahmad of *jamāl* (beauty), and that one "purely Ahmad" was sent in the latter days.
10. The Arabic idiom **al-ʿawdu aḥmad** ("the return is more praiseworthy") — so a Gospel prophecy of a *second coming* renders into Arabic as "Ahmad."

## Strand 3 — Nine arguments from Sūrah al-Ṣaff itself

*(pp. 39–53)*

| Qur'anic phrase | Argument |
|---|---|
| *hādhā siḥrun mubīn* | Opponents said his writing had "magic" that captivated readers |
| ***wa huwa yudʿā ila'l-islām*** | **The linchpin** — one can only be *called to* Islam if Islam already exists; the Prophet brought it, so this must be a later figure |
| *bi-afwāhihim* ("with their mouths") | An age of argument and *fatwās*, not the sword — the Prophet's era was one of swords |
| *mutimmu nūrih* | The *perfection* of light, tied to the hadith "how can a people be led astray at whose beginning am I and at whose end is the Messiah" |
| *li-yuẓhirahū ʿala'd-dīn* | Prevailing over all religions requires rail, telegraph, press, post; also this verse appears 3× in the Qur'an, always alongside mention of the Messiah or the Gospel |
| *hal adullukum ʿalā tijārah* | An age dominated by commerce |
| *kūnū anṣāra'llāh* | No mention of *hijrah* — whereas the Prophet's era was defined by the Muhājirīn/Anṣār distinction |
| **62:3–4** *wa ākharīna minhum* | Two raisings of the Prophet; the second is the Messiah's era |

Also: *min baʿdī* means "after," not "immediately after" — and in any case the Prophet came six centuries after Jesus, so nobody applies "immediately" literally.

# Assessment of evidential weight

**The genuinely substantive argument is one:** *wa huwa yudʿā ila'l-islām*. That is a real textual observation — the clause does sit oddly if the subject is the Prophet. But the mainstream counter is strong: *islām* in Qur'anic usage frequently carries its generic sense of submission to God (the Qur'an calls earlier prophets *muslimūn*), and most grammarians read 61:7 as a **general maxim** about liars-against-God rather than a continuing description of the same individual. It is a fair question, not a proof.

**The strongest rhetorical material is Strand 2, items 7 and 8** — but note what these actually are: *tu quoque*. Showing that Muhammad Ali's own Anjuman took *bai'at* "in the name of Ahmad" is devastating **against those specific opponents** in that specific quarrel. It says nothing about what the Qur'an means.

**Strand 2 is substantially circular** as evidence of the Qur'anic referent. Proofs 4, 5, 6 and 9 amount to: he called himself Ahmad, his revelations called him Ahmad, and he said the verse was about him. That is evidence only if one already accepts his claim to revelation. He adopted the name *because* he believed the prophecy pointed to him — so the naming cannot then serve as independent confirmation. Proofs 1–3 are decent evidence about **his own name**, a much smaller claim that nobody really contests.

**Strand 3's "signs of the age" arguments are the weakest.** Railways, telegraphs, printing presses and commercial intensity are retrofitted; no reading of the text generates them in advance, and every era can be described as uniquely commercial or uniquely contentious. The threefold co-occurrence with mentions of the Messiah is an interesting literary observation but carries no doctrinal weight on its own.

**Proof 10 (*al-ʿawdu aḥmad*) is clever but overreaches** — it is an idiom meaning "returning is better," not a name; converting it into a proper noun is a considerable stretch.

**The negative case (Strand 1) is the most respectable portion.** It is factually accurate that the Qur'an never names the Prophet "Ahmad," and the *ism*-as-attribute reading of the five-names hadith is linguistically defensible. But establishing that "Ahmad" is an attribute of the Prophet does not by itself establish who else the verse designates. That gap is bridged only by accepting Ghulam Ahmad's claim — which is precisely the thing in dispute.

## Bottom line

The case is internally coherent if one grants its starting premise, and it is genuinely effective against its immediate Lahori targets. As evidence for someone not already inside the movement, it does not close: it rests finally on accepting Ghulam Ahmad as a recipient of revelation, which is the conclusion rather than a premise.

Sunni and Shia scholarship rejects the identification, reading Ahmad as Muhammad and treating any application to a 19th-century claimant as incompatible with *khatm al-nubuwwah* (the finality of prophethood). That objection — which these pages address only obliquely, via the *burūz* doctrine — is the real crux of the disagreement, not the philology of "Ahmad."
